## Page copy

**Reformation · 1500–1600**

### Faith in an age of print

Who could interpret Scripture, teach the faith, and order the life of the Church? In sixteenth-century Europe, these questions reached far beyond universities. Sermons, translations, letters, and printed arguments brought them into homes, parishes, and courts.

The texts gathered here open onto a world of inherited devotions and contested loyalties: Christians learning to pray, weighing obedience against conscience, and asking what faith required in daily life.

**Browse the texts ↓**

**An era, many traditions.** “Reformation” names a period, not a single position. This library currently offers a mainly Protestant selection; the objects below also recall devotional practices that reformers inherited, disputed, or retained.

## The world behind the words

Three objects invite us into the period’s arguments and habits. Each is contemporary evidence, with a different relationship to the texts.

### A face in circulation

Lucas Cranach the Elder · 1520. *Martin Luther as an Augustinian Monk*. Engraving.

A printed portrait presents Luther in his monastic habit. Read beside his writing, it invites a question: how does an author become a public figure?

**Qualification:** A portrait of Luther, not a likeness for every reformer.

- [The Met record 358260](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/358260)
- [API record](https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/public/collection/v1/objects/358260)
- [Image](https://images.metmuseum.org/CRDImages/dp/web-large/DP102205.jpg)
- Rights: Public Domain on the museum page; API `isPublicDomain: true` verified.
- Alt: Engraved bust portrait of Martin Luther wearing an Augustinian habit, above an inscribed tablet.
- Reader preview: “A 1520 engraving presents Luther in his monastic habit, an early public image of the writer.”
- Reuse: era page and Luther author page. Text-level use only with a short explanation of its date and relationship.

### Prayer held in the hand

Netherlandish · early 16th century. *Half of a Prayer Bead with Jesus Carrying the Cross*. Boxwood.

Inside a carved bead, Christ carries the cross. This small object brings prayer into the hands as well as the mind, recalling a devotional world that continued amid religious change.

**Qualification:** Exceptional craftsmanship; not an everyday possession shared by all.

- [The Met record 464446](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/464446)
- [API record](https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/public/collection/v1/objects/464446)
- [Image](https://images.metmuseum.org/CRDImages/md/web-large/DP371980.jpg)
- Rights: Public Domain on the museum page; API `isPublicDomain: true` verified.
- Alt: An open, round boxwood prayer bead containing a densely carved scene of Christ carrying the cross.
- Reader preview: “A carved prayer bead recalls the material practices of devotion that formed part of the reformers’ inherited world.”
- Reuse: contextual entry for devotion and prayer. Do not assign it to Protestant authors as evidence of their personal practice. Museum describes these opening beads as rare commissions; avoid presenting it as a typical household item.

### A shared scene, disputed meanings

Albrecht Dürer · 1510. *The Last Supper*, from *The Large Passion*. Woodcut.

Christ and the apostles gather at the table. Beside texts on the sacraments, this familiar scene offers a starting point for noticing how Christians could share Scripture while disagreeing about worship.

**Qualification:** Made before 1517; not an illustration of a later doctrinal position.

- [The Met record 387750](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/387750)
- [API record](https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/public/collection/v1/objects/387750)
- [Image](https://images.metmuseum.org/CRDImages/dp/web-large/DP820407.jpg)
- Rights: Public Domain on the museum page; API `isPublicDomain: true` verified.
- Alt: A woodcut of Christ and the apostles gathered around a table in a vaulted room.
- Reader preview: “Dürer’s 1510 Last Supper offers a familiar biblical scene beside later disagreements over the sacraments.”
- Reuse: era context and selected sacramental texts. This pairing is editorial interpretation, not a museum claim that the print expresses any later confession.

**Image note:** Images: Wellcome Collection and The Metropolitan Museum of Art · Public domain. Objects illuminate a wider historical world; they are not possessions of the authors unless identified as such.

## How this scales

Keep the same slots across eras: introduction, one opening image, up to three contextual objects, up to three reading invitations, and the catalogue grid. Do not require the same topics or number of objects in every era; omit an unfilled slot rather than insert a weak match.

**API-assisted:** museum metadata and image URLs for known object IDs; dates, materials, collection credits and public-domain flags; published-text availability, accession numbers, reading times, author links, counts and controlled themes. Met API retrieval was tested for all three objects above. Wellcome image delivery was tested via IIIF; automated catalogue ingestion was not implemented or tested.

**Editorial:** object selection, interpretation, geographic and confessional relevance, uncertainty labels, image crops/fit, reading invitations and selection order. An API search result alone does not establish historical relevance or clear the rights to a different photograph.

Store each approved object once with its source ID and URL, image URL, rights evidence and checked date, historical dates/place, alt text, long caption, short reader caption, approved associations and explicit exclusions. Associate these records with eras, authors and, where useful, texts. Reuse one approved caption rather than generating a fresh paragraph per page.

**Inheritance:** era membership makes an object a candidate; it does not automatically prove relevance to every author or text. Author/text-specific selections take precedence. A broadly applicable era object can appear under “From this period” with a link to the era page. Luther’s portrait stays with Luther; the prayer bead is contextual evidence of devotional practice, not a claim about an author’s possessions or beliefs. Show at most one unobtrusive contextual entry in the reader initially, with an option to explore more.

**Map readiness:** no map is included in this equivalent prototype. The live `author_locations` table currently has no rows; current associations are mainly country-level. A country association cannot establish where an author wrote a particular text. A later map should state what its markers mean and use reviewed historical places and periods before presenting city-level precision.

## Prototype boundaries and review

Original Nicene frame preserved. Reformation desktop frame is editable, retains shared object and cover instances, and was checked in a rendered screenshot; no missing fonts reported. Source labels and the three featured reading titles have links. This is not a responsive implementation or a fully wired browsing prototype. No production code, catalogue records, or publication flags changed.

Before production, review the editorial wording, confirm the balance of voices, and implement counts and the full grid dynamically. Image selection deliberately tests reuse across a different era; it does not yet establish an engagement benefit. Evaluate whether visitors move from context into reading, and whether they return, after a measured release.

# Nicene & Post-Nicene — illustrated content draft

Editorial draft · 4 October 2026 · For discussion and Figma composition; not published.

## Opening

### Nicene & Post-Nicene

325–600

The name begins with Nicaea, where bishops gathered in 325 to debate and define the Church’s confession of Christ. The centuries that followed produced writings Christians still turn to for teaching, prayer and argument. Christianity gained imperial support, while disagreements about belief and practice continued within and beyond the Roman world.

These writings also bring us close to particular lives: a parent educating a child, a congregation listening to a sermon, a new Christian learning the meaning of baptism. The library includes theological treatises alongside letters, hymns, biblical interpretation and practical counsel. Read across them to encounter both the questions Christians shared and the different circumstances in which they answered them.

“Nicene & Post-Nicene” identifies a period in this library. “Patristic” identifies a tradition associated with the early Christian teachers often called the Church Fathers; the two categories overlap without being identical.

## The world behind the words

Section introduction: Three surviving objects offer glimpses of writing, clothing and worship in Egypt and Syria. Each belongs to a particular time and place within this wider era.

### 1. Words on a fragile surface

**Object caption:** Papyrus Fragments with Seth on Mount Sinai. Lisht, Byzantine Egypt, fifth–sixth century. Papyrus and ink. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Rogers Fund, 1934, 34.1.226.

**Interpretation:** A surviving text is also a surviving object. These inked papyrus fragments give writing a physical presence: words occupy a surface that can be damaged and broken apart. They invite us to consider the material work behind the texts we now read on screens. Their Egyptian setting is one part of this era’s much wider world. They are not a manuscript of the works presented here, but an opportunity to look closely at a writing material used during these centuries.

### 2. Clothing and the life of virtue

**Object caption:** Fragment of a Tunic. Attributed to Egypt, fourth–fifth century. Wool and linen, plain and tapestry weave. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Gift of George F. Baker, 1890, 90.5.295.

**Interpretation:** Clothing brings moral teaching into the scale of everyday life. This woven fragment belonged to a tunic: an object worn on the body rather than displayed on a page. Read beside Chrysostom’s discussion of fine clothing and children’s adornment, it gives material substance to the subject of his argument. The connection is comparative. We do not know the wearer’s faith, wealth or age, and this Egyptian garment is not evidence of what his particular listeners wore. It opens a question his text presses: what do our possessions teach us to value?

**Short preview:** An Egyptian tunic fragment offers a material comparison for teaching about clothing and possessions. Its wearer’s identity and beliefs are unknown.

### 3. Worship and remembrance

**Object caption:** Chalice. Antioch, Syria, sixth century, with twentieth-century restorations. Partly gilded silver. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Fletcher Fund, 1947, 47.100.34.

**Interpretation:** This cup connects the material practice of worship with a family’s hopes. The museum identifies its form with chalices used to distribute consecrated wine at the Eucharist, and explains its inscription as an expression of hope for salvation after death. Beside the era’s writing about sacraments, it reminds us that theological language belonged to acts performed with people and objects. This is a sixth-century Syrian example, later than both Augustine and Chrysostom. It helps us explore the period’s worship without reconstructing either author’s own church.

## Where to begin

### A short first reading

**Leo the Great — Sermon 21 (On the Feast of the Nativity)**

Begin with a brief sermon in which the meaning of Christ’s birth becomes a call to live differently. It offers a compact encounter with theology spoken to a congregation.

https://www.thestromata.com/texts/sermon-21-on-the-feast-of-the-nativity

### Faith in the household

**John Chrysostom — Address on Vainglory and How to Bring Up Children**

A longer reading on ambition, possessions and the formation of a child. Notice how the argument moves from public admiration into the habits of the household.

https://www.thestromata.com/texts/address-on-vainglory-and-how-to-bring-up-children

### Understanding worship

**Ambrose of Milan — On the Mysteries**

Follow an explanation of baptism and the Eucharist that moves between scriptural stories and material actions. A useful entry into the relationship between Christian teaching and ritual.

https://www.thestromata.com/texts/on-the-mysteries

**Recommendation for this pilot:** one small, manually approved set of entries.
